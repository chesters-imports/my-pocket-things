Uncle Chester owned an antique shop down on 54th and Broadway. A tiny little face of a shop, one you might never notice considering its small size.  All of the other big office buildings and shopping centers clogged the roads, towering above the tiny shop.  The outside walls were painted green so many years ago that it was peeling away to show the previous old yellow it was when first built. The front consisted only of a small door and a even smaller window, and a creaky old sign that merely read “Chester’s Imports”. Around it was a small lawn, 4 feet all the way around the shop, with a single flower blooming.

On summers when she was in high school, Samantha would work in the shop, dusting off the various pieces, adding polish to the higher-end, newer woods. On the weekends she would sometimes help him with the books, entering the few sales they received into a ledger who’s end number just continued to turn more red as the weeks went on.

The amount of money that Uncle Chester made (or in this case, did not happen to make at all) never seemed to bother him, and he never even batted an eye at the offers he often got from the town on a nearby businesses who were eager to buy his plot of land and merge it into a tall super building just like the rest on these stretches of the city.  Sam has seen a few of these offers during those weekends and it was always shocking that he didn’t take them.

Chester often went on trips to other places, different states or even different countries, looking for amazing finds. Whenever he was away, he would simply close to shop, hanging a small sign outside that read “Out looking for finds! Will be back when we’re back!” Sam never understood how he could afford these constant trips with the lack of sales, even once going so far as to ask her mother about his income level.

Her mother merely shrugged her motherly shrug and said, “I’m not sure, my love. Perhaps he won the lottery and never told anyone.”

Time had passed since Sam last worked in Chester’s Imports. She’d gotten older, graduated high school and moved away to go to college and learn about the world.  Since they were so close, he would often send her letters detailing his exploits as he searched for these “finds” he loved so much. Old wood and rusted brass knobs, strange lamps in interesting configurations, hand-crafted chairs made of bent metal and stitched fabrics. Stories about riding elephants and walking through jungles and of funny old ladies will silly old accents who are preparing to die by ridding themselves of beautiful antique material possessions.

She adored these notes so much and would often write back about the mundaneness of educations, of various school parties she was too tired to attend because really, she cared deeply about her studies and her education and the classic future she hoped to have.

One afternoon, after returning from a particularly boring class, Sam returned to her dorm room to see she had gotten a package. Her roommate must have placed it on her bed. She tossed aside her bookbag and pulled the keys out of her left pocket, cutting excitedly into the shipping tape. It was a package from her uncle, who had never sent her anything this large before. 

Throwing back the flaps of the box, she discovered it will filled with small green packing peanuts and a note floating right on top.  The note was in a red envelope, with the word “Samantha” written in silver ink. Turning it over, she discovered it has been sealed with Uncle Chester’s wax-method. Sam always thought it was a silly habit, but it certainly made getting mail from him feel like a very important matter. 

Never liking to break the seal, she tore the letter open along the top, pulling out the white paper from inside. It was a hand written letter and it said:
 
Dear Samantha,
I am sending you this most important gift, guard it with your life.  I will have to be cryptic and hope you are able to discover the same thing I have discovered. I cannot give you any more information then this. I am sorry this letter is so short. Please, look at your gift. I hope you can come home soon.
Love,
Uncle Chester
 
Samantha was confused. She scratched her head, reading the words a few times to see if some how she was missing something.  She’d never know Chester to be the cryptic type, in fact he was usually a straight shooter, if not sometimes a bit of an exaggerator.  She took a minute to just glance at the box, wondering what could possible be inside that could somehow cause such an odd-ball letter.  
*Only one way to find out*, she thought and she began digging into the box filled with packing peanuts.

Casey lived for homework.  He knew that sounded weird and probably his sister and all of the kids at school who thought he was cool would laugh, but he really did enjoy it. Something about the writing and reading and thinking and repeating really appealed to him. He excelled in all of his classes and go constant As. Its funny how in school, when you are popular, somehow it is unpopular to actually do well at the very reason you’re at school to begin with: learning.

Tonight’s homework seemed interesting but just as he was about to settle into Chapter 5: Internal Organs and Their Functions his cellphone rang. *What a terrible invention, *he thought to himself. *Intrusive, always being available all the time.* It of course was his mother, not a call he could avoid. 

“Heyo, ma!”

“Casey…” the voice came back. She sounded sad. His shoulders slumped. “Casey… Uncle Chester is dead.”
There was what seemed like forever of a pause.

“Can you tell your sister?” 
 
---
 
The annoying thing about packing popcorn is that it flows everywhere when you reach into a large box. *I’ll probably be finding popcorn for months,* she thought as she got her hands around a rectangular object, pulling it out into the open air. 

What she saw was an absolutely beautiful box. Hand carved from the finest mahogany wood with various stone inlays around the outside of it. The center was slightly inset with what appeared to be a hand-painted picture of a small cabin.  Although completely normal looking by all accounts, something about the painting seemed terribly off. Sam ran her finger, feeling the grooves left behind by the strokes of the paintbrush. 

*What an absolutely beautiful box, *she thought, turning it upside to see what engravings might be left behind. Often with these classic boxes you will find some sort of engraved signature or otherwise. What she found on the underside was mildly perplexing. It said:
 
CHESTER’S IMPORTS
 
Absolutely strange, she thought. How could this antique box be made by Chester’s Imports, her own uncles shop. That just doesn’t make any sense. Turning the box back upright, she ran her fingers across the front, over the hinge keeping the box closed. It was a golden hook which tucked into another golden loop.  She thumbed at is gentle, slowly raising the hinge. Just as it was almost fully unlatched, her phone rang. It was Casey, and it wasn’t good news.
 
---
 
She didn’t think about opening the box right away. It was a whirlwind of sadness and travel as she returned back home for the funeral and the reading of the will. She had always been close to him, and she was crushed it had been almost 2 years since they’d spent any amount of time together. Now she would never get to see him again, and that was utterly devastating.

A day after the funeral, she and her father (her uncles brother) went to Chester’s Imports, primarily to take inventory of his “estate”, but also to make sure there was no loose ends and to be nostalgic. Sam wasn’t sure how she would feel returning to this place, having not been here in more years then it had been since she’d last seen her uncle. 

It was funny how some things never changed in this place. The same two armoires sit against the wall behind the register, somehow managing to overflow with paper in the drawers, additional messes of papers and records hidden behind the double doors. The cash register was old, the classic kind that made all sorts of noises as you entered the totals and added up the purchase. Sam slid her fingers over the till button, the drawer springing out and hitting her in the gut. Inside the drawer was two 20 dollar bills, a fortune from a fortune cookie that read “Tomorrow morning, at the first chance turn left” and a photograph of Uncle Chester, Sam and her little brother Casey, posing in front of the shop. It was old and worn by this point, like it had been touched and looked at quite often.

Sam’s eyes filled with the sadness as she brushed her thumb over her uncle’s face. She would greatly miss him, that was for sure. 

“Hey Sam!” came her father’s voice from the back of the store. “Come here honey, I have something you might want to check out.”

Navigating Chester’s Imports was always something that Sam was good at, ever since she was a little girl. There was almost no order to the way things were presented: stacks of books on top of tables on top of other tables, couches turned to face the walls with bookshelves behind them. Sometimes you had to limberly maneuver your way through the angular edges of the beautiful artifacts he found and collected here. Sam did this with skill and ease, only occasionally stopping to note something new that had been added or something missing that must have been sold. It wasn’t much, but she saw two new armchairs that were absolutely gorgeous. She wondered if she might get to take a few items to remember him by.

“What’s up?” she asked as she reached the deep-back of the shop. “What did you find?”

He handed her another envelope. This envelope was the same red color as the one she had gotten on the day she got the terrible news about his passing. On the front was the same **Samantha** written in silver pen. The back was stamped with the same exact wax stamp. 

“Huh.”

“I guess he planned to mail this to you?”

“Probably. Odd.”

“What’s odd about it?”

“Well, I hadn’t mentioned… it’d just all been so sudden, but Uncle Chester had sent me a package right before we found out he was gone.” She flipped the envelope over in her hands, examining the front and back.

“Oh?”

“Yeah. And there was a red envelope just like this one inside, and a very beautiful box that was of course locked.”

“Well the one I just found seems to have something inside.” She looked down. She hadn’t even noticed, but he was absolutely right. There was a bulge in the envelope. She tore the envelope open in her usual manner, pulling out the same style white paper from inside. On the paper there is a small golden key, taped with way too many pieces of clear tape. The handwriting on the paper read:
 
Sam,
A key for the box. The box is a key.
 
“What does it say?” her dad asked as she looked up, a confusion clearly painted on her face. He crinkled his nose as he read it, glancing back up after a second. “Guess you’ll have to try the key on the box?”
“Guess so.”

Nothing more was found as they looked through the shop, and she put it out of her mind for the rest of that week. The box was still at college, so there was no sense harping on the mystery. Eventually, even her dad seemed to forget the letter all together.
 
---
 
That night she dreamt about a cabin, one that she felt she knew, felt vaguely familiar to her.  A sea of green mossy grass surrounded it and she suddenly realized she was barefoot, standing in that grass, wiggling her toes. The sun felt warm, life giving, unlike the warmth of the sun on any of the most beautiful days she had ever experienced. The outside of the cabin even had a green tint of moss, urging her to approach, to touch the soft mossy exterior. 

The cabin itself was modest made of wood that had the bark removed tediously by hand. The roof sloped gently, held up by 3 steady two-by-fours. The exterior offered little embellishments - A simple door, and one simple window. A small rocking chair sat on the porch, facing out into glorious fields that seemed to stretch on forever. She managed to reach the door before she woke up, but it was locked.
She woke up with the erie feeling that she’d been somewhere real, that she knew this cabin and yet she felt unbelievably calm. She wished she could fall back asleep.
 
---
 
The next two weeks went by slowly.  Sam was desperate to get back to school, the tired monotony of her family, the constant pull of this person, or that person. The constant dinners with family. She felt sluggish, caged. She’d packed her bags days before the train trip, spending most of the last hours staring out into the distance, often thinking about the cabin she’d begun dreaming about almost every night since she’d visited Chester’s Imports. She thought about the mossy ground and the curves of the nearby trees, providing gentle shade that was never needed but was so easy to enjoy. She thought about the door, how it always was locked. She imagined what beautiful, simple things could be inside.

She thought about napping on the small wooden porch, maybe taking up a hobby like knitting or crocheting to give her more productive reasons for just relaxing. It felt, in her dreams, like the most important place on Earth, except the Earth part she sometimes felt wasn’t even a certain. Something about the peacefulness, the green mossiness that was unbelievable soft, it just didn't feel like any thing she’d ever felt.

*Certainly its only unearthly because its in a dream, *she considered and figured to herself that it was probably the truth. By this point one thing was a certain, she almost ached to sleep some nights to experience that place.

The train ride home was entirely uneventful. She read the last few chapters of a book she needed to finish for school, occasionally taking time to stare out of the window at the landscape as it zoomed by.  She chewed on her fingernails and contemplated needing to break that habit if she ever wanted to enjoy nail polish again.  And she thought about the cabin, the cabin, the cabin. 
 
---
 
Once she returned back to school, things feel back into their normal pace.  She had tests and studies and homework and all of the usual things you would expect a college student to have, and she was immediately sucked into that whirlwind of classrooms and number 2 pencils and note taking and lecture listening. She didn’t dream about the cabin, which was at first disappointing, but then the memory of the cabin itself subsided and she couldn’t remember the feeling of the moss or the shapes of the roof anymore. She couldn’t remember the small river that ran right in front of it, moss covered log long since collapsed over it, providing a safe passage for anything that might want to cross. Then it was almost as if she couldn’t remember the cabin anymore.

One afternoon, she was in desperate need of a pen. The one she was using had run out of ink and she was only half way through writing the outline for her report. She sighed, rummaging through her desk drawers. Paperclips, whiteout, erasers. No, no, no. Stamps, envelopes, not what was needed. 

To the right side of her desk she kept a filing cabinet. It was bright yellow, covered with stickers of stars and kittens. It had been the dorm room’s previous occupant’s and Sam found good use for it with her own items so she never got rid of it. The top, smallest drawer contained nothing but a few empty notepads and a green sharpie which was definitely not the right type of pen for the job. The second drawer down was filled with mail she had received, letters from home, her cellphone bills, receipts for recent purchases she worried she might have to return. 

The last drawer was stuck. 

“That’s odd,” she thought out loud. It wasn’t stuck the last time she opened it, when she put that funny box Chester has sent her before she’d found out he passed. She tugged again, and it wouldn’t budge. “Pesky old thing,” she laughed, wiggling the handle. This particular filing cabinet’s bottom drawer was lockable. Somehow it must have accidentally become locked the last time she closed it. 

“Damn it,” she swore aloud, frustrated that she had potentially locked the gift Uncle Chester had sent her, the last contact she would ever get to have with the man she considered to be her second father. Since the filing cabinet was not hers there was no way for her to unlock it. Defeated, she resumed her search for a pen, eventually finding one and completing her assignment barely before midnight.

That night she dreamt of the cabin again, and the fresh smells of moss and lilies floating through the magnificent air.
 
---
 
“So, he sent you a box?” Casey asked. He was visiting Sam for the weekend, taking in the city. He was getting close to college age and their parents wanted them to attend the same college that she was already attending. A trip to see his sister, they thought, was sure to convince him to attend this school over another he’d shown interest in. It was too far away, they said. How would he ever be able to visit home? 

“Yup, and I was opening it up right when you called with the news.”

“Damn. So you never got inside?”

“Nope.”

They fell silent. Casey thought about Uncle Chester and all of the firecrackers and noisemakers he’d brought him from all over the world. He was really like a second dad to both of them.  Sam pushed her fries around with a fork, staring out at the clouds.

“And a key too?”

“Yup. With that odd little note.” She motioned towards the paper beside his plate, the one that read “A key for the box. The box is a key.”

“Did the box even need a key?” The question took Sam by surprise. She thought back to the day she had received it, thumbing at the golden clasp, staring at the ghost-like painting on the box. What was the painting even of? She couldn’t remember anymore. 

“No, no I don’t think it did.”

“Its really too bad you’ve locked the thing up!” Casey teased. He knew how much the locked drawer was driving her insane. She had found the key and the note inside her luggage just the day before, while finally finishing to unpack. It reminder her of the locked drawer, of Uncle Chester. 

She sighed.

“It sure is.”

Another period of time passed where the siblings barely said a word.  Casey took bites out of his burger, occasionally checking his phone for push notifications from the 20-some-odd social networks you now had to be a part of if you were a high school student.  Sam thought about the exam she had coming up on Tuesday and how much she had to study but she wouldn’t get the chance to because Casey was down. She was just deciding to accept flunking the class when Casey broke through the silence.

“Did you try the key Unc’ sent in the filing cabinet?”

Sam laughed. That was the most absurd idea she felt he’d ever said, and he’d had some doosies.  

“I’m serious!” he said, pushing the note about the key back to her. “It says the key is for the box, but you said the box didn’t even have a lock.”

“That’s your evidence?” she snorted, a childish giggle eeking out of her lungs. “You are reaching, Case. Too much SyFy channel, I think.”

Casey was unfased. “Dead serious sis, why not? Stick it in there, give it a twist. Worst case I am wrong, best case—“

“There is no best case,” she interjected. “It will obviously not work.”
 
---
 
The returned to her dorm room a few hours later, having ate their fill of food and aimlessly window shopped. Casey convinced her to buy him ice cream, a comic book and a box of noisemakers, “for Chester!”. 

“What are you, 10?” she asked in the checkout line. He shrugged and laughed. It was one of the best times they’d spent together since they were kids. She was heartbroken he had to get on the train to return home the following morning.

Sam through her bag on the couch, flopping down next to it. She thought about her pending exam again, feeling the pangs of panic over her impending failure before pushing it out of her mind again. No sense crying over spilt milk.

Casey, on the other hand, was thinking of their earlier conversation.

“Where’s the key?” he said. Sam looked up from her stressful thinking and laughed. “I am serious Sam! Where’s that key! Lets try it!”

He was already knelt down beside the filing cabinet, fumbling with the keys from his own pocket.
“Hell, maybe one of mine will work. These things are pieces of shit anyways.” He jammed a few of his smaller keys into the hole, none of them fitting right. 

“Come ooooooon, Sam!” he shouted, “Lets just try.”  She smiled in spite of the stupid idea, figuring why the hell not give it a try. He seemed really into the game of it, and she could never live with herself being a complete party pooper after the fun day they had.

“Fine, fine,” she chuckled, getting off the couch. “I’ll nab it.”

It was shoved deep in her bookbag. A bad place for a key when the bag is full but it was the safest place she knew of it. She pulled it out, this time giving it a really good look. It was a small golden key, with an oddly smooth and rounded teeth. The handle of the key was a round metal ring with a thin golden crescent moon inside. A small blue gem hung from a small chain off the end of the crescent moon. 

“There is no way this key is working on that ugly yellow thing,” she said as she walked over to where he brother kneeled. He held up his hand, looking up at her with a childish glee, a deep hope that this would work. She placed the funny little key in his hand, patting it twice as if it will somehow comfort him when they key doesn’t even fit. Or maybe she hoped it would fit, that the mystery that came across in her uncle’s original letter or even the letter that came with the key, that those things were not the ramblings of a man she’d never get to see again but instead a doorway into something… more.

Casey put the key to the lock, looking back up at his sister as he grinned cheekily. The key slide right into the lock. It turned, unclasping the yellow filing cabinet’s lock, without resistance. Like they were made for each other. 

Casey’s grin quickly shifted into one of complete surprise. 

Sam thought about the cabin.
